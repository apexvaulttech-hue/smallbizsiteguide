How Much Should a Small Business Pay for a Website in 2026?

The short answer

Most small businesses should expect to pay between $300 and $5,000 for a website. The honest median for a service business with five to twenty pages is somewhere around $1,200. A simple brochure site built on a modern tool can genuinely cost under $500. A custom build with booking, payments and copywriting can run past $10,000.

If a quote comes in far outside those bands, the question is not whether it is expensive. It is what you are actually paying for.

What each price band actually buys

$0 - $300A free builder plan on a subdomain, or a template you set up yourselfTesting an idea, or a business not yet trading

The most common mistake is not overpaying. It is paying $2,500 for something that took four hours to build and used a $60 template.

The costs nobody puts in the quote

A website quote rarely covers everything. Budget for these separately:

  • Domain name — roughly $10-$20 a year. Buy it yourself, in your own name, always. If there is one line item worth insisting on, it is this one.
  • Hosting or builder subscription — $0-$40 a month depending on the platform. This is the recurring cost most people forget to ask about.
  • SSL certificate — should be free. If someone quotes you for this, treat it as a warning sign about the rest of the quote.
  • Email at your domain — $5-$10 per user per month. Worth having. A Gmail address on a business card looks improvised.
  • Photography — $0-$200. Free stock libraries are genuinely fine for most local businesses. Pay for a photographer only if you sell something people need to see.
  • Content — the real hidden cost. Writing your own page text takes six to ten hours. Hiring it out runs $100-$300 a page.
  • Maintenance — $0-$100 a month. Sometimes worth it, often it is just updates you would run yourself in five minutes.
Three-year total for a $1,200 site: roughly $1,900-$2,400 once hosting, domain and email are counted. Worth knowing before you sign, not after.

When a $300 template beats a $3,000 custom build

A template wins when all four of these are true:

  1. You sell one thing to one kind of customer.
  2. You need five pages or fewer.
  3. You, or someone on your team, will spend a weekend on it.
  4. Your customers find you through Google Maps, word of mouth, or a van with your number on it — not through the website itself.

That is a plumber, an electrician, a mobile hairdresser, a personal trainer, a dog walker, a locksmith, a small accounting practice. For those businesses, a clean template with your photos, your prices, your service area and a phone number at the top does the entire job.

A plumber does not need custom design. A plumber needs to be findable and to look legitimate once found. Paying $4,000 for bespoke design and getting fourteen enquiries a month is not better than paying $600 and getting fourteen enquiries a month.

When custom actually pays for itself

Pay for a custom build when the website does work, not when it merely exists:

  • You take bookings or appointments and need scheduling built in.
  • You sell products and need a real checkout.
  • Several competitors have strong sites and you need to outrank them.
  • Customers compare you side by side before choosing, so design carries real weight — restaurants, salons, clinics.
  • You need to connect to software you already run: inventory, CRM, accounting.

If none of those apply, put the money into Google Ads or a better van instead. Both will return more per dollar than prettier typography.

Do not pay for these

  • "SEO packages" bundled into a new site build. A brand-new site has no authority yet. There is very little for an SEO package to work with in month one.
  • A separate SSL certificate fee. It is free in 2026. This is the fastest way to spot a quote built on padding.
  • A rebuild you did not ask for. If someone tells you your site is dangerously out of date and quotes $3,000, get a second opinion before agreeing to anything.
  • A "proprietary system" the developer keeps control of. If you cannot leave with your content and your domain, you are not buying a website. You are renting one.
  • Long contracts. Month-to-month is standard for hosting and for builders.

Ask these five questions before you pay anything

  1. Whose name is the domain registered in? It must be yours. Not the developer's, not the agency's.
  2. Can I have the login details when the project ends? The answer has to be yes. If it is not, walk away.
  3. What happens to the site if I stop working with you? It should keep working.
  4. What is the monthly cost after launch, and what happens if I stop paying it?
  5. Can I export my content? On most modern builders, yes. Get it in writing anyway.

Someone who will not answer those five clearly is selling you a rental, not a website. That is the single most useful ten minutes you will spend in this whole process.

A simple way to decide

Ask one question: does my website need to persuade people, or just prove I exist?

  • Prove I exist — trades, single-service businesses, anyone found through Maps or referrals. Spend $300-$800. Build it yourself or use a cheap template.
  • Persuade people — restaurants, salons, clinics, anything compared side by side. Spend $1,500-$4,000. Design and copywriting genuinely matter here.
  • Do work for me — bookings, sales, integrations. Budget properly and expect to pay for maintenance indefinitely.

Most small businesses are in the first group, and are being sold to as though they are in the third. That mismatch is where the money goes.

The one-line version

If you take nothing else from this page: spend $300-$800 on your first site, own your domain, and prove the business works before you spend more.
