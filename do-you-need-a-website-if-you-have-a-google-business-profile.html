Do You Actually Need a Website If You Have a Google Business Profile?

The short answer

For some local businesses, no — a well-maintained Google Business Profile genuinely covers what you need, and a bad website is worse than none. For most, you need both, because the profile gets you found and the site convinces the person who found you.

This is not the answer most people writing about websites will give you, since they sell websites. So here is the honest version, including the cases where the answer is no.

What a Google Business Profile does well

  • Shows you on Maps. When someone searches "plumber near me", your profile is what surfaces. A website does not outrank a profile in the local pack.
  • Shows your hours, phone and location. The three things most local searches want.
  • Collects reviews. The single strongest ranking factor in local search, and the one thing that moves enquiries most.
  • Shows photos of your work. Enough for a trade to demonstrate competence.
  • Gives people a one-tap call button. For a mobile business this is the entire conversion.
  • Costs nothing. There is no paid tier for any feature you need.

If your business is a trade, a mobile service, or anything where the customer decides in one phone call, that list may be everything you need.

When a profile alone is genuinely enough

A website is unnecessary if all of these describe you:

  1. You have one service or a short, obvious list.
  2. You serve a defined local area.
  3. Customers contact you by phone or message, not by form or booking.
  4. Nobody needs to see your pricing before calling.
  5. You have 20+ reviews and a rating above 4.5.

That is a locksmith, a tow truck, a mobile mechanic, a house cleaner, a window fitter, a dog walker. For those businesses, spending $800 on a website instead of spending that time getting ten more reviews is a poor use of money.

If you are in this group, the honest advice is: get your profile right first, and revisit the website question in a year.

Two rows decide it for most people. Ranking for what you do rather than where you are — a page on "emergency boiler repair" can bring you searches a profile never will. And ownership: your profile lives on Google's terms, and it can be suspended, edited by competitors, or changed by an algorithm.

The businesses that need both

  • Anything compared before choosing — restaurants, salons, clinics, photographers, venues. People browse several, and browsing happens on a website.
  • Anything with a range of services — a builder who does extensions, renovations and repairs needs somewhere to explain each.
  • Anything selling to other businesses — commercial buyers look for a site and treat its absence as a signal.
  • Anything taking bookings — a profile cannot run a diary.
  • Anyone whose profile is doing badly — if you are invisible on Maps despite having a profile, a website is part of the fix, not a replacement for it.

If you skip the website, do this instead

An unmaintained profile is worse than no profile. If you are relying on yours, treat it as your website:

  1. Fill every field. Services, service area, hours, attributes, opening date. Empty fields are wasted ranking.
  2. Add ten real photographs. Your work, your van, your team. Not stock.
  3. Ask every customer for a review. A text the same day works. This is the highest-value hour you will spend.
  4. Answer every review, including the bad ones, briefly and without arguing.
  5. Post occasionally. Monthly is enough. Silence looks like a closed business.
  6. Claim it properly and check nobody else has access.

If you build the site, connect the two

A website and a profile work as a pair, and linking them is free:

  • Put the profile's link on your site's contact page.
  • Put the site's URL in the profile — the website field is a ranking signal.
  • Use the same business name, address and phone number in both, character for character.
  • Put your reviews on the site, or a link to them.

Inconsistency between the two is a common and entirely avoidable problem. If your site says "Smith & Sons Plumbing" and your profile says "Smith and Sons Plumber", you are working against yourself.

The test that settles it in one minute

Search for your main service plus your town, in a private window. Then ask:

  • Can you find yourself in the first three results? If yes and you have 20+ reviews, a site may not be urgent.
  • Does a competitor with a website outrank you? Then you have found the gap.
  • Is there a paid ad above you? Someone in your market thinks those customers are worth money.
  • Would you call yourself based on what you see? This is the honest one, and it is usually the answer.

The one-line version

Run your profile properly whether or not you build a site. Then build the site when you need to explain more than one thing, take a booking, rank for what you do rather than where you are, or look credible to a buyer who does not know you yet.
