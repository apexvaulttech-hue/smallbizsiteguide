How to Move Your Website Off Wix or Squarespace Without Losing Google Rankings

The short answer

You will not lose your rankings, provided you do one thing properly: redirect every old page address to its new one. Content that moves and is redirected keeps its standing. Content that moves without redirects starts again from nothing, which is why so many migrations are followed by months of silence.

The whole job is one checklist, done in one order.

Why migrate at all

People move for four reasons, and only some are good ones:

  • Cost. You are paying for a tier you do not use. Good reason.
  • Need. You need bookings, a shop, or something the platform will not do. Good reason.
  • Speed or control. The platform cannot be made faster or shaped further. Usually good.
  • Restlessness. You read that another one is better. Usually not a good reason. Migration carries risk and costs a weekend, and if the current site works, that weekend is better spent elsewhere.

If your site does what you need it to do, do not migrate it. There is no prize for being on the fashionable platform.

What actually affects rankings

ChangeEffect on rankings
Moving to a new platform, same URLs, properly redirectedMinimal and temporary
Moving and redirecting old URLs to new onesMinimal if redirects are one-to-one and permanent
Moving without redirectsSevere. You lose the standing of every page.
Changing your domain nameDepends. Can be handled, but it is the riskiest part.
Speeding the site upPositive, if the content is unchanged
Rewriting the page titles and textCan go either way. Rewriting loses the phrases you ranked for.
Changing to a new design with identical contentLittle effect. Search engines rank pages, not aesthetics.

The pattern: search engines care about addresses and content, not platforms. A beautifully rebuilt site that drops its old URLs will do worse than the ugly one it replaced.

The migration checklist, in order

Before you touch anything

  1. Export your content. Most platforms let you export pages, posts and images. Do it even if you plan to rebuild by hand — you will want the old text.
  2. Write down every URL. Every page, post, image and file. Your sitemap is the fastest source: it is usually at yoursite.com/sitemap.xml. Save that list somewhere you will still have it in a month.
  3. Note your current traffic. Screenshot your analytics for the last 90 days, per page. Without this you cannot tell whether the migration worked.
  4. Note your rankings for your five most valuable search terms.
  5. Do not migrate during a busy season. Inconvenience now beats inconvenience in your best month.

During the move

  1. Keep the same URLs wherever possible. If your new platform can produce /contact instead of /contact-us-2, make it.
  2. Rebuild content faithfully. Same headings, same key phrases. This is not the moment to rewrite your copy — that is a separate change, made later, on its own.
  3. Redirect everything that changed. One redirect per old page, pointing at the matching new page. A 301 redirect is the permanent one; that is what you want.
  4. Redirect to the specific page, never the homepage. Sending every old URL to your front page tells search engines the content is gone, and it is worse than doing nothing.
  5. Keep the old site running until the new one is verified. Publish the new one, check it, then switch.

Immediately after

  1. Test twenty redirects manually. Paste an old URL into a browser and confirm it lands on the right new page. Not the homepage — the right page.
  2. Resubmit your sitemap in Google Search Console.
  3. Run the site through a broken-link checker. Old internal links pointing at addresses that no longer exist are a common miss.
  4. Update your Google Business Profile with the new URL.
  5. Update every directory, social profile and email signature. Anywhere the old address appears.
  6. Leave redirects in place for at least a year. Permanently, ideally. They cost nothing.

How long it takes to recover

Expect a dip, and expect it to be temporary. Two to six weeks of softer traffic is normal even when everything is done correctly. If traffic has not recovered by three months, something is wrong — usually a missing redirect or a block in robots.txt — and it needs finding rather than waiting out.

Two weeks of dip is a normal migration. Two months of dip is a mistake.

The five mistakes that cause most damage

  • Redirecting everything to the homepage. The most common and most harmful.
  • Forgetting images and PDFs. They have their own addresses, and they can carry traffic.
  • Accidentally blocking the new site. A staging site left set to "discourage search engines" can silently kill everything.
  • Deleting the old site too early. Keep it until you are sure.
  • Migrating and redesigning at the same time. If something goes wrong you will not know which change caused it. Do one, wait, then do the other.

Where to move to

If you are leaving for control and speed, a static site is worth considering: it is faster and cheaper than any traditional platform, and hosting can be free. It suits content and brochure sites well, and less well anything needing a login or a database.

The one-line version

Keep your URLs, redirect everything that changes, one page to one page, and leave those redirects up forever. Do that and a migration is a weekend's work with a fortnight of soft traffic. Skip it and you are starting the site again.
